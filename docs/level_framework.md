# Level Framework — Meridian Silicon

**Version:** 0.2
**Owner:** Total Rewards
**Purpose:** The canonical leveling rubric. The leveling agent applies this document. Every leveling decision in the system must cite specific factors and anchors from here.

---

## 1. Company profile

| Attribute | Value |
|---|---|
| Business | Fabless semiconductor products + accompanying software platform and applications |
| Headcount | ~1,500 |
| Model | Design and IP owned in-house; fabrication outsourced to foundry partners |
| Sites | US (HQ + design center), EU, India (largest engineering center), LATAM |

**Confirmed:** fabless. No owned fab, no manufacturing operator population, no shift differentials or hourly structure.

## 2. Track structure

Two career tracks, equal in status. Movement between tracks is lateral, not promotional.

### Individual contributor: L1–L8

| Level | Title |
|---|---|
| L1 | Associate Engineer |
| L2 | Engineer |
| L3 | Senior Engineer |
| L4 | Staff Engineer |
| L5 | Senior Staff Engineer |
| L6 | Principal Engineer |
| L7 | Distinguished Engineer |
| L8 | Fellow |

### Manager: M3–M7

| Level | Title | IC equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| M3 | Manager | L4 |
| M4 | Senior Manager | L5 |
| M5 | Director | L6 |
| M6 | Senior Director | L7 |
| M7 | Vice President | L8 |

**Confirmed:** M3 ↔ L4. Manager levels map one level above the matching IC number, reflecting that a first-line manager owns team delivery — broader scope than a Senior Engineer. Equivalence governs range assignment and internal equity comparison, not title.

L1–L3 have no manager equivalent. People management begins at M3.

**L8 is populated with exactly one incumbent.** This is deliberate: the level must be real enough that the agent can retrieve precedent, but rare enough to be credible. The single Fellow anchors L7–L8 leveling and is a useful edge case for the equity agent, which must not treat a population of one as a distribution.

## 3. Leveling factors

Seven factors. Every leveling decision must produce a rating on each applicable factor.

| # | Factor | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Scope of impact** | Whose work is affected — task, team, function, business unit, company |
| 2 | **Autonomy** | How much direction is received; who reviews the work |
| 3 | **Problem complexity** | How well-defined the problems are; how much ambiguity must be resolved |
| 4 | **Technical depth / breadth** | Mastery within a discipline, or span across disciplines |
| 5 | **Ownership scope** | Scope of the artifact, process or book owned — see applicability below |
| 6 | **Influence** | Who this person persuades and how — peers, adjacent teams, executives, industry |
| 7 | **Span & budget** *(manager track only)* | Team size, structure depth, budget authority |

**Experience is not a factor.** Years of experience is a sanity guardrail, never a determinant. If a leveling decision rests on experience, it is wrong.

### Factor 5 applicability by family group

Factor 5 has three variants. The variant is selected by family group, and the leveling agent must state which variant it applied.

| Family group | Variant | What is owned |
|---|---|---|
| Silicon and software engineering | **Product lifecycle ownership** | Block → subsystem → product → platform → roadmap |
| Corporate and shared services | **Process and program ownership** | Task → process → function-wide program → enterprise policy |
| Go-to-market | **Revenue responsibility** | Supporting role → individual quota → territory → segment → region |

Factor 5 is never marked not-applicable and never dropped. A six-factor decision and a seven-factor decision are not comparable, and allowing the agent to skip the factor is how corporate roles drift out of calibration with engineering roles at the same level.

## 4. Level anchors

### Scope of impact
- **L1–L2** — Own assigned tasks. Impact contained within the immediate team.
- **L3** — Own a defined component or workstream. Impact felt across the team.
- **L4** — Own a subsystem or complex workstream. Impact across the function.
- **L5** — Own an area spanning multiple teams. Impact across the function and adjacent functions.
- **L6** — Impact across the business unit. Decisions shape multiple product generations.
- **L7** — Impact across the company. Recognized authority beyond the company in the discipline.
- **L8** — Impact on company strategy and on the industry.

### Autonomy
- **L1–L2** — Work is assigned and reviewed in detail.
- **L3** — Works independently on defined problems; outcomes reviewed, approach not.
- **L4** — Sets own approach on ambiguous problems; consulted rather than directed.
- **L5** — Identifies what should be worked on within an area.
- **L6** — Sets direction for a domain; accountable for outcomes without oversight.
- **L7–L8** — Defines direction where none exists. Judgment trusted without review.

### Problem complexity
- **L1–L2** — Problems are well-defined with known solution paths.
- **L3** — Problems are defined; solutions require judgment among known approaches.
- **L4** — Problems are ambiguous; solution paths must be constructed.
- **L5** — Problems span domains and involve competing constraints.
- **L6** — Problems are strategic and open-ended; framing the problem is part of the work.
- **L7–L8** — Problems have no precedent inside the company or in the field.

### Technical depth / breadth
- **L1–L2** — Developing proficiency in one discipline.
- **L3** — Proficient in one discipline; delivers reliably without support.
- **L4** — Deep in one discipline; credible in adjacent ones.
- **L5** — Authority in one discipline; works effectively across several.
- **L6** — Authority across multiple disciplines; sought out company-wide.
- **L7–L8** — Recognized externally. Publications, patents, standards bodies.

### Factor 5a — Product lifecycle ownership *(engineering families)*
- **L1–L2** — Contributes to a block or module under direction.
- **L3** — Owns a block or module end to end.
- **L4** — Owns a subsystem across a full development cycle.
- **L5** — Owns a major subsystem or a full derivative product.
- **L6** — Owns architecture for a product line across generations.
- **L7–L8** — Owns platform or roadmap direction spanning product lines.

### Factor 5b — Process and program ownership *(corporate families)*
- **L1–L2** — Executes defined tasks within an established process.
- **L3** — Owns a discrete process end to end; handles exceptions within policy.
- **L4** — Owns a process spanning teams; designs the process, not just runs it.
- **L5** — Owns a function-wide program; sets policy within the function.
- **L6** — Owns a portfolio of programs; sets policy affecting multiple functions.
- **L7–L8** — Owns enterprise policy; represents the company externally on the discipline.

### Factor 5c — Revenue responsibility *(go-to-market families)*
- **L1–L2** — Supports revenue activity without individual quota.
- **L3** — Carries an individual quota against a defined account set.
- **L4** — Carries a significant quota or owns a defined territory.
- **L5** — Owns a territory or segment; involved in strategic accounts.
- **L6** — Owns a region or major segment; shapes go-to-market strategy.
- **L7–L8** — Owns global revenue strategy for a business.

### Influence
- **L1–L2** — Communicates within the team.
- **L3** — Influences peers through demonstrated work.
- **L4** — Influences adjacent teams; represents the team to partners.
- **L5** — Influences function-level decisions; drives cross-functional alignment.
- **L6** — Influences business-unit strategy; credible with executives.
- **L7–L8** — Influences company strategy; represents the company externally.

### Span & budget *(manager track)*
- **M3** — 4–10 direct reports, all individual contributors. No budget authority beyond requisitions.
- **M4** — 10–25 people; may include a team lead. Owns a program budget.
- **M5** — 25–70 people across teams; manages managers. Owns a functional budget.
- **M6** — 70–200 people; multiple layers. Owns a sub-functional P&L input.
- **M7** — 200+ or a critical function of any size. Executive-level budget accountability.

**Span alone does not determine manager level.** Scope of impact and problem complexity govern; span is corroborating evidence.

## 5. Leveling rules

1. **Scope of impact is primary.** When factors split across two levels, scope of impact is the tiebreaker.
2. **The lower level governs a split.** If a role rates at two adjacent levels, assign the lower — unless scope of impact is unambiguously at the higher level.
3. **Deep-but-narrow does not reach L6.** Technical depth without cross-domain breadth and organizational influence caps at L5.
4. **External recognition is required for L7 and above.** Not optional, not inferable from internal reputation.
5. **Manager level is not automatic from headcount.**
6. **Title in the source document is evidence, not input.** Level from described scope, never from the title.
7. **Hybrid roles level at the dominant discipline**, with the secondary discipline noted for market pricing.
8. **State the factor 5 variant applied.** A decision that does not name the variant is incomplete.
9. **When confidence is below threshold, escalate.** Output the two candidate levels and the specific factor that would resolve it.

## 6. Source organization calibration

Applies when leveling roles from outside Meridian — acquisitions and external hires.

**The principle:** scope language is relative to the organization that produced it. A Director at a 40-person company and a Director at a 40,000-person company describe different jobs using the same word. The framework handles this correctly only if the parser extracts *absolute* scope evidence rather than relative language.

- Relative, unusable: "senior member of the analog team", "led a large program"
- Absolute, usable: "owned PMIC subsystem across three tapeouts shipping 4M units", "managed 8 engineers and a $2.1M budget"

### Source organization context

Every external population carries a context record:

| Field | Values |
|---|---|
| `source_headcount` | integer |
| `source_stage` | seed / growth / late-stage private / public |
| `source_type` | whole company / carve-out / asset purchase |
| `parent_headcount` | integer, where the source is a carve-out |
| `org_depth` | levels between IC and CEO in the source org |
| `platform_dependency` | low / medium / high — see below |

### Expected drift

The context sets a prior on how source titles will map. **This is a flag, not an adjustment.**

| Source archetype | Expected drift | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Small company, under ~150 people | Titles map **down**, often 1–2 levels | Title inflation used in place of compensation; shallow org depth means fewer distinct scopes |
| Peer-sized company | Roughly neutral | Comparable calibration |
| Carve-out from a much larger parent | Mixed — may map **up** on scope, **down** on breadth | Larger absolute scope, but narrower role boundaries |

### Rules

1. **The prior never adjusts the level.** No size multipliers, no automatic minus-one for startups. Level from evidence.
2. **The prior is used to check the result.** When the evidence-based level is consistent with the prior, confidence rises. When it contradicts the prior, escalate — that is the signal the prior exists to produce.
3. **Platform dependency must be assessed for carve-outs.** A verification engineer supported by a central CAD team, methodology group and shared validation infrastructure has narrower effective breadth than the title implies. High platform dependency caps factor 4 at the level the person could sustain without that support.
4. **Carve-outs can map up or down, and both must remain available.** Do not encode a directional assumption. Absolute scope pushes up; platform dependency pushes down; the reviewer agent surfaces the tension rather than resolving it silently.
5. **Org depth is corroborating evidence for manager levels.** A source org with three levels between IC and CEO cannot contain an M6 no matter what the title says.

## 7. Crosswalk negotiation

Applies during acquisition integration. The acquired organization has its own framework and a legitimate interest in the mapping outcome. The system models this explicitly rather than assuming the acquirer is always right.

### Participants

| Role | Reference document | Objective |
|---|---|---|
| **Crosswalk agent** | This framework | Produce the evidence-based mapping |
| **Acquired-side advocate** | The acquired company's own framework | Contest mappings where the acquired framework supports a different outcome |
| **Arbiter** | This framework | Rule on contested mappings |
| **Equity agent** | Meridian incumbent data | Veto revisions that break internal equity |

### Admissible arguments

The advocate may argue only from evidence and from documented framework language.

- **Admissible:** scope evidence not reflected in the original mapping; misapplied factor variant; a factor anchor the crosswalk agent read incorrectly; comparable precedent in Meridian's own population.
- **Inadmissible:** the source title; retention risk or morale; what the person was paid; seniority or tenure; what peers at the acquired company received.

Retention risk is real, but it is a compensation remedy, not a leveling argument. It is handled by the retention agent through retention awards or red-circling — never by moving a level.

### Arbiter standard

The arbiter applies this framework, not a midpoint between the two positions. Splitting the difference is not an available verdict. The expected outcome is that most original mappings are upheld; an advocate win rate above roughly one third indicates the advocate is over-weighted or the arbiter is under-anchored, and that ratio should be reported in evals.

### Equity gate

Every revision passes to the equity agent before it is final. A revision that places an acquired employee above Meridian incumbents with demonstrably greater scope at the same level is rejected and returned to the arbiter, with the conflicting incumbents named.

### Verdicts

| Verdict | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Upheld** | Original mapping stands |
| **Revised** | Mapping changed; equity gate passed |
| **Red-circled** | Level upheld at the lower placement, current pay protected above range maximum |
| **Escalated** | Unresolved after the round limit; routed to a human with both positions stated |

Red-circling is the realistic resolution for most genuinely contested cases and should be available to the arbiter as a first-class outcome, not a fallback.

### Round limit

Two rounds maximum. After the second arbiter ruling, unresolved cases escalate. All contested cases — regardless of verdict — are written to an **exception register** with both arguments, the ruling, and the governing rule cited.

## 8. Job families

Twelve families. Sub-families exist where market pricing differs meaningfully within a family.

### Silicon engineering
| Family | Sub-families |
|---|---|
| Digital Design | RTL Design, Microarchitecture |
| Analog & Mixed-Signal | Analog Design, RF, Power Management |
| Physical Design | Place & Route, Timing, Signal Integrity |
| Design Verification | Functional Verification, Formal, Emulation |
| Silicon Validation & DFT | Post-Silicon Validation, DFT, Test Engineering |

### Software engineering
| Family | Sub-families |
|---|---|
| Embedded Software | Firmware, Device Drivers, RTOS |
| Platform Software | Compilers & Toolchains, SDK, Systems Software |
| Application Software | Applications, Web Platform, Software QA |

### Product, systems and go-to-market
| Family | Sub-families |
|---|---|
| Systems & Architecture | Systems Architecture, Applications Engineering (FAE) |
| Product & Program | Product Management, Technical Program Management |
| Go-to-Market | Sales, Sales Engineering, Marketing |
| Corporate | Finance, HR, Legal, IT, Operations |

**Why sub-families matter:** analog and RF designers price differently from digital designers at the same level, and both price differently from application software engineers. The system must not average across a family when pricing.

**Corporate roles price against general industry surveys, not technology surveys.** A Senior Payroll Analyst in San Jose is priced against general industry, not against the semiconductor peer set. This is a market pricing rule, not a leveling rule — corporate roles level on the same seven factors as everyone else, using factor variant 5b.

## 9. Pay mix and sales compensation

Level and family together determine pay mix. The system models three components: base, target variable, and equity.

### Standard mix — engineering, corporate, product

| Level | Target bonus % of base | Equity participation |
|---|---|---|
| L1–L2 | 5% | Limited |
| L3–L4 / M3 | 10% | Standard |
| L5 / M4 | 15% | Standard |
| L6 / M5 | 20% | Enhanced |
| L7 / M6 | 25% | Enhanced |
| L8 / M7 | 30–40% | Executive |

### Go-to-market mix

Quota-carrying roles use a base/variable split rather than a bonus percentage, and the split widens with level.

| Role type | Base / variable split | Plan type |
|---|---|---|
| Sales Engineering (non-quota) | 80 / 20 | Bonus on team attainment |
| Sales, individual quota | 60 / 40 | Commission on attainment |
| Sales, territory | 55 / 45 | Commission with accelerators above 100% |
| Sales leadership (M4+) | 65 / 35 | Overlay on team attainment |
| Marketing | Standard mix | Bonus |

### Modeling requirements

- **On-target earnings (OTE) is the comparison unit for sales roles**, not base salary. Range placement, compa-ratio and market comparison for quota-carrying roles all operate on OTE. Comparing a salesperson's base against a base-salary range is a real and common error, and the system must not make it.
- Sales ranges are expressed as OTE ranges with the split stated.
- Actual variable pay varies by attainment; the incumbent data carries both target and actual so the equity agent can distinguish a low earner from a low performer.
- Equity for quota-carrying sales roles is materially lower than for engineering at equivalent level. This is a real source of cross-family tension and worth modeling honestly.

## 11. Decisions

### Resolved
- Manager mapping: M3 ↔ L4
- Fabless; no manufacturing population
- L8 populated with one incumbent
- Sales compensation modeled with real base/variable splits and OTE-based comparison
- Corporate families retained, leveled on factor variant 5b, priced against general industry

### Open
1. **FX handling** — fixed rate table or dated rates? Fixed is simpler and defensible for a demo; dated is more realistic and affects the acquisition cost model.
2. **Equity valuation** — model equity at grant value, or attempt an expected value? Grant value is simpler and probably sufficient.
3. **Acquired company framework** — the negotiation in section 7 requires the acquired company to have a real, differently-shaped framework. Proposed: MTS I–V with no separate manager track. Confirm before the data model spec.
